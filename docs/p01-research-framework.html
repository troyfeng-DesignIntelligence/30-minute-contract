01 · Background

The problem this research is trying to understand

Platform delivery systems promise users a 30-minute window. This promise is not made by the rider — it is made by the algorithm. But when the window is at risk, the pressure falls entirely on the rider, who must absorb the uncertainty through their own body and decisions.

Riders operate under two distinct types of pressure simultaneously: algorithmic pressure (time remaining, penalty for late delivery) and social pressure (user messages, urge notifications, perceived disappointment). These pressures interact in an environment of fundamental information incompleteness — riders cannot know road conditions ahead, restaurant preparation progress, or whether a shortcut is safe.

This project uses a game-based research method to study how these pressures interact with information availability to produce risky decisions. The theoretical grounding is Oulasvirta's computational rationality framework: behavior as utility maximization under cognitive and environmental constraints, modeled via Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs).

03 · Research Question

The question this study asks

Core Research Question
In food delivery decision-making, does information incompleteness amplify the effect of social pressure (user urging) on risk-taking behavior — and if so, by how much?

This question is grounded in POMDP theory: in a partially observable environment, agents must maintain a belief state — a probability distribution over possible true states. When information is scarce, belief states are wide and uncertain. The question asks whether social pressure has greater influence on decisions precisely when the belief state is most uncertain.

The answer has direct implications for platform interface design: if social pressure only amplifies risk under information scarcity, improving information transparency may be sufficient to reduce dangerous riding. If social pressure amplifies risk regardless of information availability, the urging mechanism itself must be redesigned.

04 · Hypotheses

What we expect to find

Social pressure (user urging) will increase risk-taking behavior significantly more under low information conditions than under high information conditions.

As information completeness increases across the three rider levels, the baseline rate of risk-taking (without social pressure) will decrease — but not to zero, due to residual uncertainty from random events.

Social pressure has equivalent effect on risk-taking regardless of information level — in which case, platform information design is insufficient and the urging mechanism itself must be eliminated.

05 · Game Design

How the study is conducted

Players take the role of a delivery rider. Each session lasts under 5 minutes (real time), representing a compressed delivery run. The game is built in Unity with a simplified 2D city map. Players accumulate experience across runs — not as ability upgrades, but as information upgrades: the city becomes more legible, route probabilities become more visible. The rider's physical capability never changes; only their knowledge of the environment does.

Decision Nodes

Each run contains 8–12 decision nodes. At each node, the player chooses between a safe option (costs time) and a risky option (saves time, probabilistic negative outcome). All probabilities are hidden from the player — they must be inferred from context and accumulated experience.

Social Pressure Trigger

At randomized intervals during each run, a user message appears on screen. The message varies in urgency: neutral check-in → mild impatience → explicit urging. The message has no effect on actual game mechanics — it cannot cause a penalty. Its only function is psychological. The system records whether the player's next decision is more risky than their baseline for that information level.

06 · Bayesian Model

How the model works

Two Bayesian processes run simultaneously — one inside the game world (the rider's belief state), one outside the game world (the system's model of the player).

Layer 1: In-game Belief State (POMDP)

At each decision node, the rider maintains a belief over the true state of the world. The belief is updated with each observation using Bayes' rule:

Belief Update Rule
b'(s') ∝ P(o | s', a) · Σ P(s' | s, a) · b(s)
b(s) Current belief — probability distribution over possible world states
a Action taken (wait / run red / take alley / etc.)
o Observation received after action (road clear / congested / accident)
P(o | s', a) Observation model — how likely is this observation given the true state
b'(s') Updated belief — new probability distribution after this observation

The information level (new/experienced/veteran) directly sets the width of the initial belief distribution — veterans start with narrower, more confident distributions. Social pressure does not change the belief distribution; it changes the utility function the player uses to select actions.

Layer 2: Player Model (External Bayesian Inference)

The system maintains a model of each player's latent risk preference θ, updated after every decision:

Player Model Update
P(θ | decisions) ∝ P(decisions | θ) · P(θ)
θ Latent risk preference parameter (continuous, 0 = fully risk-averse, 1 = fully risk-seeking)
P(θ) Prior — initialized as uniform; all risk preferences equally likely
P(decisions | θ) Likelihood — how probable are these decisions given this risk preference

Update Cycle

01
Decision node reached
Player faces safe vs. risky choice under current information level
02

After 5–10 runs (roughly 30–50 minutes of play), the model has enough data to produce a reliable estimate of the player's risk preference under each condition, and the interaction effect between information level and social pressure.

07 · Design Implications

What the results will mean for platform design

The study is designed to produce one of two distinct design conclusions, depending on which hypothesis is supported:

If H1 is supported
Social pressure amplifies risk only under information scarcity
→ Platform intervention: improve real-time information transparency for riders (kitchen progress, road conditions). Urging notifications can remain but must be paired with better information delivery.
If H3 is supported (null hypothesis)
Social pressure amplifies risk regardless of information level
→ Platform intervention: the urging/notification mechanism must be removed or fundamentally redesigned. Information improvements alone are insufficient to reduce dangerous riding behavior.

Both outcomes are meaningful. The research is not designed to confirm a predetermined conclusion — it is designed to produce evidence that distinguishes between two different platform design strategies.
